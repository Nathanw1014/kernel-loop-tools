The Harbour Bridge at Calder Point

The first plan for a crossing at Calder Point was drawn in 1874 by a surveyor named Ellen Marsh, who had been sent by the colonial works office to find out whether the narrows could carry anything heavier than a rope ferry. Her report, eleven pages long and written in a careful slanting hand, concluded that a timber trestle would survive no more than six winters in water that moved that fast, and that a stone bridge would cost more than the whole district raised in taxes in a decade. The office filed the report and kept the ferry.

For the next thirty years the ferry was run by the Okafor family, who bought the licence in 1881 for forty pounds and a promise to cross at least twelve times a day between March and October. Samuel Okafor kept a ledger of every crossing, and the ledger survives in the town library. It records cattle, wool bales, a piano that went into the water in 1893 and was recovered the next morning, two weddings performed on the deck because the church on the far side had flooded, and one crossing in July 1899 that took four hours because the cable snapped halfway over and the boat drifted almost to the lighthouse.

By 1905 the town of Calder had grown to nearly three thousand people, and the ferry could no longer keep up with the timber trucks from the mill at Wren Hill. A committee led by the mill owner, Harriet Vance, and the schoolmaster, Peter Lindqvist, raised money for a new survey. The engineer they hired, Tomas Reyes, had built two railway bridges in the north and believed the narrows could be crossed with a single steel arch if the footings were sunk into the rock shelf on the eastern side rather than into the mud on the western side. His drawings show an arch of one hundred and sixty metres, with a roadway hung beneath it on steel rods.

Work began in the spring of 1909. The eastern footing went down without trouble, but the western side turned out to have far less rock than Reyes had measured. Divers found a layer of clay nearly nine metres thick above the shelf, and the contractors spent most of 1910 driving timber piles through it. Two workers, brothers named Aldo and Marco Bellini, died in October of that year when a coffer dam gave way during a storm. A small stone with their names still stands near the western approach.

The arch was closed on the 14th of March 1912, and the bridge opened to traffic on the 2nd of September the same year. The opening ceremony was delayed by an hour because the ribbon, which had been ordered from the city, arrived on the last ferry the Okafor family ever ran. Samuel's daughter, Grace Okafor, was invited to cut it. The town paper reported that she declined the scissors and untied the knot instead, saying that her father had never cut a rope he could untie.

The bridge carried horses, carts and the occasional motor car until the 1930s, when the mill trucks grew too heavy for the original roadway. In 1936 the deck was replaced with reinforced concrete and the hangers were doubled, work that closed the crossing for seven months and brought back a temporary ferry run by a company from the city. Older residents complained that the new ferry kept no ledger and never crossed on Sundays.

The bridge was painted grey in 1951, green in 1967, and grey again in 1988 after a public vote in which green lost by forty-one votes. In 2004 an inspection found corrosion in eleven of the hangers, and the state government proposed demolishing the arch and building a concrete beam bridge alongside it. The town objected. A petition with more than six thousand signatures, roughly twice the town's population at the time, argued that the arch was the only reason most visitors stopped in Calder at all. The government agreed to repair it instead, at a cost that was never published.

Today the bridge carries about nine thousand vehicles a day. The Okafor ledger is kept in a glass case in the library, open at the page for the 2nd of September 1912, where the last entry, in Grace's handwriting, reads simply: final crossing, one ribbon, no charge.
